Sample process

Each sample should answer a question.

Sample names and requirements can vary by buyer. Agree the purpose, inputs and approval authority for every round.

  1. Development sample

    Tests the initial interpretation of the design, materials and construction.

  2. Fit revision

    Applies consolidated comments to measurements, proportion and shape.

  3. Colour or finish review

    Confirms the required shade, print, embroidery, wash or other surface treatment.

  4. Approval sample

    Records the version accepted for the next commercial or production decision.

  5. Pre-production alignment

    Checks production materials and specifications before bulk work begins.

  6. Reference control

    Keeps approved samples and comments connected to the final order information.

Tech pack

What a garment tech pack should contain.

A tech pack is the document that turns a design into a sample request. It does not need to be perfect on day one, but the gaps should be marked so they can be resolved during clothing sample development.

What a garment tech pack should contain.
Sketch and referencesTechnical flat sketch, reference images and the intended use of the garment.
MeasurementsBase size, size chart, measurement points and tolerance expectations.
Bill of materialsFabric composition, GSM, structure and finish, plus trims, labels and packaging.
ConstructionSeam types, stitch details, neckline and hem finishes, pockets and fastenings.
Artwork and decorationPrint or embroidery files with dimensions, placement, colours and technique.
Labelling and packingMain label, care label, hangtag, folding and carton requirements.

Product-specific guidance: T-shirts, hoodies and sweatshirts and womenswear and kidswear. After approval, quality is followed through production quality control.

Common questions

Sampling questions from buyers.

Can development begin from a reference image?

Yes, but a reference image does not contain all technical details. Measurements, materials, construction, artwork and quality expectations still need to be defined.

How many sample rounds are required?

That depends on the product complexity, completeness of the first brief, material availability and the clarity and speed of buyer comments.

Does sample approval automatically confirm bulk production?

No. Bulk production should begin only after the buyer and sourcing team align the approved sample, specifications, materials, commercial terms and production plan.

What is garment sampling?

Garment sampling is the stage where a development sample is made from your tech pack or reference, reviewed for fit, materials and workmanship, and revised until it can be approved as the production reference.

Do I need a tech pack before I start?

A tech pack helps, but you can start with clear references and mark what is undecided. The team will identify which specifications are needed before sampling or costing.

How is a sample different from bulk production?

A sample proves the design, fit and materials. Bulk production needs the approved sample, specifications, materials and commercial terms to be aligned, and quality checks are agreed for the order.

Sample approval records

Make the next revision unambiguous.

A clear comment sheet connects the sample you reviewed to the changes you expect. Keep one consolidated version for each round so that fit, artwork and material decisions do not become separate instructions with conflicting dates. Use the following fields as a starting point for your own approval record.

Make the next revision unambiguous.
Identify the sampleRecord the style code, sample round, date, size and material references. Note any substitute fabric or trim. State the purpose of the review, such as fit, construction or artwork, so approval of one feature is not interpreted as approval of the entire garment.
Describe the changeFor each point, record the existing result, the requested change and the reference to follow. Add annotated photographs or a measurement sheet where useful. Replace comments such as improve the fit with the exact area and revised measurement or reference.
Record the decisionMark each point as approved, revise or awaiting information, and identify who must close it. Keep the approved points visible in later rounds. If a previously approved feature changes, make that change explicit instead of silently replacing the reference.
Prepare bulk handoverBring together the final specification, approved sample identification, fabric and trim references, artwork, labels and packing details. Record outstanding items and the authority needed to close them before the production plan is confirmed.

For example, a sleeve-length revision should show the reviewed sample, its measured result and the new target using the agreed measurement method. The next review can then check the same point. This record becomes part of the production and quality handover; it does not replace written order confirmation.

Sample types

What each sample is meant to prove.

Every sample should answer one question. Calling them by the right name keeps approvals clear.

Proto or first sampleChecks the design and pattern. Fabric may be a substitute. Judge silhouette and construction, not final colour.
Fit sampleMade in the real or closest fabric on the base size. Judge fit, measurements against the size chart and handfeel.
Size setThe full size range made from the same pattern. Judge grading, neck openings and proportions at both ends of the range.
Pre-production sampleMade from bulk fabric and trims. Judge colour, print, trims and workmanship against the signed-off reference.
Shipment samplePulled from the finished lot. Judge whether bulk matches the approved reference before goods leave the factory.

First samples take 7 to 14 days, and two sample rounds are included in a quotation. Sample and courier charges are payable by the buyer, and the sample cost may be adjusted or refunded against a bulk order where agreed.

Common causes of extra rounds

Why samples go round more than twice.

Most revision rounds come from a short list of avoidable causes.

  • Measurements given without points of measure or tolerances.
  • Fabric GSM and shrinkage not stated, so the first sample uses a different cloth.
  • Print or embroidery approved on paper, not on the real fabric.
  • Comments sent in pieces from several people instead of one consolidated list.
  • Trims chosen late, after the pattern is already fixed.
