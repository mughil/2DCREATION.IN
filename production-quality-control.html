Quality checkpoints

Review quality while action is still possible.

The inspection plan, method, sampling level and acceptance criteria must be agreed for each order. Website information does not replace a buyer-approved quality protocol.

  1. Pre-production review

    Confirm approved references, specifications, materials and known risk points.

  2. Early production check

    Observe whether the line is following construction, measurement and workmanship requirements.

  3. Inline follow-up

    Monitor recurring issues, corrective actions and the status of affected production.

  4. Finishing review

    Check relevant appearance, pressing, measurement, label and finishing requirements.

  5. Packing verification

    Review approved folding, assortment, labelling, carton and documentation inputs.

  6. Final status

    Record inspection findings and any decision required before shipment release.

Evidence-based follow-up

What a useful quality report should show.

Reports should help the buyer understand what was checked, what was found, what changed and what remains open.

  • Order, style, colour, size and inspected quantity identification
  • Approved reference and specification version used for review
  • Measurements, workmanship, appearance and packing observations
  • Photographs or other evidence linked to the recorded finding
  • Corrective action, owner, timing and recheck status when required
  • Clear final status and any buyer decision still outstanding

Common questions

Production and quality basics.

Is quality control the same for every order?

No. The product, construction, buyer requirements, risk level and applicable testing or inspection protocol determine the appropriate quality plan.

What happens when a quality issue is found?

The issue should be documented, assessed for scope and cause, assigned a corrective action and rechecked where appropriate. Any commercial or shipment decision remains subject to agreed buyer authority.

Can production begin before every approval is closed?

Starting with open approvals creates risk. Any exception should be clearly documented, assessed and authorised by the responsible parties before work proceeds.

What does garment inspection involve?

Garment inspection compares production against the approved sample and specification. It typically covers measurements, workmanship, appearance, labels and packing, recorded with photographs and a pass or fail against the agreed standard.

What is the difference between inline and final inspection?

An inline inspection checks production while it is running, so recurring problems can be corrected. A final inspection checks the finished, packed order against the agreed AQL before shipment release.

Should quality requirements be agreed before production?

Yes. The inspection stages, sampling level and acceptance criteria should be agreed before bulk production starts. See product development and sampling for how references are approved.

From observation to action

Keep an issue traceable until it is closed.

An inspection finding is useful when it points to a requirement and a next action. Agree how issues will be recorded and who can approve a correction or shipment decision. A photograph on its own does not show the affected quantity, the specification used or whether the correction has been checked.

Keep an issue traceable until it is closed.
Finding and referenceIdentify the order, style, colour, size, production stage and approved reference. Describe the observation and attach measurements or photographs as appropriate. Keep an appearance comment distinct from a recorded measurement outside the agreed tolerance.
Affected scopeRecord what was checked and what remains unchecked. Identify any material lot or production batch associated with the finding. Avoid treating a small checked sample as proof that every piece has the same result; follow the agreed inspection plan when defining further checks.
Action and recheckAssign the correction, responsible person and target date. Record the action taken and the outcome of the recheck against the original requirement. If the proposed correction changes an approved feature, route that decision to the agreed approval authority.
Packing and handoverConnect the final quality status to the approved size assortment, labels, packing and shipment information. Keep unresolved items visible in the handover record and identify the person responsible for the release decision.

For example, a measurement finding should remain linked to the style specification and inspected size through correction and recheck. A completed action record helps distinguish an issue that was reported from one that was resolved. Bring the latest sample approvals into the quality plan before production starts.

Inspection stages

Where quality is checked, and against what.

Quality control is a sequence, not a final gate. Each stage catches a different type of problem.

Pre-production meetingWalks through the signed-off sample, tech pack, trims, packing and test requirements with the factory before cutting.
Fabric inspectionChecks shade, GSM, shrinkage, holes and pilling on the fabric before it is cut.
Inline inspectionChecks seams, measurements and workmanship while sewing is under way, when problems can still be fixed.
Final inspectionChecks a random sample of the finished lot against the agreed AQL standard before packing is closed.
Pre-shipment checkConfirms packing, labelling, cartons and quantities match the order.

Reading an inspection

AQL and defect classes in plain terms.

AQL, the acceptable quality limit, sets how many defects are tolerated in the sample drawn from a lot. Agree it in writing before production.

Critical defectA defect that could harm the wearer, such as a loose small part or a broken needle fragment. Usually zero tolerance.
Major defectA defect that makes the garment unsellable or noticeably worse, such as a wrong size or open seam. A common general limit is AQL 2.5.
Minor defectA small flaw that does not affect use or sale, such as a loose thread. A common general limit is AQL 4.0.

We apply AQL 2.5 for major defects and AQL 4.0 for minor defects. Inspection is carried out by factory QC, or by an independent third-party inspector when required, and a report follows 24 to 48 hours after inspection.
