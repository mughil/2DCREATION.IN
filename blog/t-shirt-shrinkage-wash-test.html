T-shirt shrinkage and wash testing: a buyer checklist

T-shirt shrinkage and wash testing for apparel buyers: what to measure, how to run a simple wash test, how to set a tolerance and why bulk must be checked too.

Written by the 2D Creation sourcing team in Tirupur, India, who coordinate product development, sampling, production follow-up and quality control for international apparel buyers.

A T-shirt that looks right on the table can change size in the first wash. Knitted jersey relaxes when it is washed and dried, so shrinkage and twisting are the most common reasons a sample passes review and a bulk order does not. This guide explains what to test, how to run a simple wash test on a sample, and where to set the tolerance.

Key takeaways

  • Test a washed sample, not just a dry one, before approving a T-shirt.
  • Check length and width shrinkage, side-seam twisting, neck rib recovery, print durability and colour bleeding.
  • Set the wash method and shrinkage tolerance in the tech pack, so everyone measures the same way.
  • Repeat the check on bulk production, not only on the approved sample.

Why jersey shrinks

Knitted fabric is stretched and held under tension during knitting and finishing. When the garment is washed, the yarn relaxes and the fabric moves back towards its resting shape. Length usually changes more than width, and the combination can make side seams twist round the body. Fabric that has been compacted or pre-shrunk at the mill moves less, which is why it should be named in the brief.

What to test on a T-shirt

Wash test checks for T-shirts
Dimensional changeChest width, body length and sleeve length measured before and after washing.
Twisting (spirality)Side seams that rotate towards the front or back after washing.
Neck rib recoveryWhether the neckline springs back or stays stretched out.
Print and decorationCracking, peeling or fading after wash and rub.
ColourBleeding onto a white trim or neighbouring panel, and fading.

How to run a simple wash test

  1. Measure the dry sample at fixed points and record the numbers. Mark the points with a photograph or sketch.
  2. Wash and dry the sample following the care instructions in the brief.
  3. Let the sample rest flat, then measure the same points again.
  4. Calculate the change: (before − after) ÷ before × 100.

For example, a chest width of 52.0 cm that measures 50.7 cm after washing has shrunk by 2.5%. Record the result against the tolerance in the tech pack, and photograph the twisting and the neckline.

Test methods

Laboratories commonly work to published washing and dimensional-change methods, for example ISO 6330 for domestic washing and drying, ISO 5077 for dimensional change, AATCC 135 and ISO 105-C06 for colourfastness to washing. Which method applies is a buyer and laboratory decision, so confirm it before testing rather than assuming one.

Setting the tolerance

There is no single correct figure. The acceptable shrinkage depends on the fabric, the fit and the buyer’s standard. Put the number and the test method in the tech pack, apply it to every sample round, and use the same approach for bulk. A tolerance that is agreed after the problem appears is hard to enforce.

Test the bulk, not only the sample

An approved sample proves the sample fabric. Bulk fabric is a different lot, so shrinkage should be checked again on the bulk fabric before cutting and on finished garments during inspection. Frequently asked questions

How much shrinkage is acceptable for a T-shirt?

It depends on the buyer’s standard, the fabric and the fit. Agree a tolerance and a test method in the tech pack before sampling, rather than relying on a general rule.

Does pre-washing the fabric remove shrinkage?

Pre-shrunk or compacted fabric moves less in the first wash, but it does not guarantee zero change. Confirm the result on a washed sample.
