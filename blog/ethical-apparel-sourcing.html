Ethical Apparel Sourcing: What Buyers Should Check

Ethical apparel sourcing explained: labour, chemicals and traceability checks, how audits differ from certificates, and what to ask a factory before you order.

Written by the 2D Creation sourcing team in Tirupur, India, who coordinate product development, sampling, production follow-up and quality control for international apparel buyers.

Ethical apparel sourcing means buying garments from factories that treat workers fairly, manage chemicals and waste responsibly, and can show you where and how your order was made. This guide explains what the term covers, what audits and certificates do and do not prove, and what to ask a factory before you confirm an order.

Key takeaways

  • Ethical sourcing covers working conditions, chemicals and the environment, and traceability, not one certificate.
  • A product test such as OEKO-TEX checks the garment; a social compliance audit checks the workplace. They answer different questions.
  • Ask which units will make your order, and agree in writing that printing, embroidery and washing stay in named facilities.
  • Your own buying habits matter: realistic lead times and late approvals are a common cause of excessive overtime.

What ethical apparel sourcing covers

Working conditions. Safe buildings, fair pay, reasonable working hours, no child or forced labour, and a way for workers to raise complaints.

Chemicals and the environment. Control of dyes and finishes, wastewater and waste handling, and testing for restricted substances in the finished garment.

Traceability. Knowing which factory, and which subcontractors, made each part of the order, and being able to document it.

Fair buying practices. Orders planned with realistic lead times, stable specifications and timely approvals. A factory cannot protect working hours if a style changes a week before shipment.

Audits and certificates answer different questions

Social compliance programmes such as amfori BSCI, SMETA (Sedex) and WRAP look at how a factory is run. Product and material standards look at what the garment is made of. For example, OEKO-TEX tests textiles for harmful substances, the Global Organic Textile Standard (GOTS) covers organic fibre and processing, and GRS covers recycled content.

No single logo covers everything. An OEKO-TEX label says nothing about working hours, and an audit report says nothing about whether a fabric passed a chemical test. An audit is also a snapshot: check its date, scope, findings and whether problems found were corrected. Read more in our guide to how to verify garment factory certificates.

Questions to ask a factory before you order

  • Which audit or certificate do you hold, in which facility, and when does it expire?
  • Which units and processes will make my order, including printing, embroidery, dyeing and washing?
  • Is any part of the order subcontracted, and to whom?
  • What are normal working hours and how is overtime recorded?
  • How do workers raise a complaint, and how are findings from past audits closed out?
  • How are chemicals managed, and which tests will you run on my garments?

Keep subcontracting under control

Unapproved subcontracting is one of the most common gaps in ethical sourcing: the audited factory takes the order and an unaudited unit does part of the work. State in your order terms which facilities may be used, and ask for approval before any change.

Frequently asked questions

Is a certificate enough to call a supply chain ethical?

No. A certificate covers a defined scope at a specific facility on specific dates. Ethical sourcing also depends on audits of working conditions, subcontracting control and your own buying practices.

What is the difference between a social audit and OEKO-TEX?

A social audit reviews working conditions in a factory. OEKO-TEX tests textile products for harmful substances. Neither replaces the other.

Does 2D Creation hold these certificates?

No. 2D Creation is a sourcing agent. Certifications belong to the factories and are checked per factory before order confirmation.

How often should I recheck a factory’s documents?

For every new order and whenever a certificate or audit is close to expiry.
