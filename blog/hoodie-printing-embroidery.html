Hoodie printing and embroidery: what to approve before bulk

Hoodie printing and embroidery for apparel buyers: decoration methods, how fleece affects results, placement and what to approve and wash-test before bulk.

Written by the 2D Creation sourcing team in Tirupur, India, who coordinate product development, sampling, production follow-up and quality control for international apparel buyers.

Print and embroidery are where a hoodie most often goes wrong. A design that looks right on a flat artwork file has to survive thick, textured fabric, seams, pockets and repeated washing. This guide explains the common decoration methods, how fleece changes the result, and what to approve before bulk production.

Key takeaways

  • Choose the decoration method for the fabric and artwork, not the other way round.
  • Always test on the actual hoodie fabric. A swatch of a different fabric proves nothing.
  • Specify size, placement, colours and technique in writing, with positions measured from fixed points.
  • Review the decoration after washing, not only on the dry sample.

Common decoration methods

Hoodie decoration methods compared
Screen printInk pushed through a screen, one screen per colour. Suits solid colours and larger runs. Plastisol and water-based inks feel and wear differently, so name the ink type.
Puff and specialty printsRaised or textured effects. They need a firm surface and a test for cracking after washing.
Heat transferA printed film or transfer applied with heat. Suits small runs and detailed artwork, but edges and stretch need checking.
Digital (DTG) printInk printed directly on the garment. Suits detailed, multi-colour artwork in small quantities. Results depend on the fabric surface.
EmbroideryStitched logos and lettering. Suits chest and sleeve logos. Fabric thickness and stitch density need to be matched.
Appliqué and patchesCut fabric or badges stitched on. Check edge finish, backing and wash behaviour.

How fleece changes the result

Brushed fleece has a soft, raised surface on the inside, and the outer face can be slightly textured. For printing, a surface that is not smooth can affect ink coverage and fine detail. For embroidery, thick fabric can let stitches sink into the pile, which can make small lettering hard to read. Stabiliser or backing is usually needed, and it affects how the garment feels inside. French terry behaves differently again. See fleece vs French terry for the fabric differences.

Placement and construction

A hoodie has more obstacles than a T-shirt. Pockets, zips, seams, the hood and rib can all interfere with a design. Give placement as measurements from fixed points, for example the distance from the neck seam to the top of the artwork, for the base size. State whether placement moves across the size range, and check how the design sits over the pocket or zip when the garment is worn.

What to approve before bulk

  1. A strike-off or embroidery sample on the real hoodie fabric.
  2. Colour approval against a reference, with the colour system named.
  3. Placement and size on the base size, and on one other size.
  4. Hand-feel, meaning how stiff or heavy the decoration feels.
  5. A washed sample, checked for cracking, peeling, fading, puckering and shrinkage around the decoration.

Record each approval against the style so it can be compared with bulk. See product development and sampling for how approvals are recorded, and apparel quality control for the checks during production.

Frequently asked questions

Is embroidery or print better for a hoodie?

It depends on the artwork and fabric. Embroidery suits simple logos and lettering, and print suits larger or multi-colour artwork. Test both on the actual fabric before deciding.

Why does print crack after washing?

Common causes are ink that does not suit the fabric, insufficient curing, or a heavy deposit on a stretchy fabric. A washed sample before bulk is the way to find this out.
