Fleece vs French terry: how to choose for hoodies and sweatshirts

Fleece vs French terry explained for apparel buyers: how they differ, which suits hoodies and sweatshirts, and what to put in the tech pack before sampling.

Written by the 2D Creation sourcing team in Tirupur, India, who coordinate product development, sampling, production follow-up and quality control for international apparel buyers.

Fleece and French terry are the two fabrics most hoodies and sweatshirts are made from, and they are often confused. They look similar from the outside. The difference is on the inside of the fabric, and it changes warmth, weight, hand-feel and how the garment behaves after washing. This guide explains the difference and how to specify the right one in a tech pack.

Key takeaways

  • Fleece has a brushed, napped inner face. French terry has unbrushed loops inside.
  • Fleece feels warmer and softer inside. French terry is lighter and more breathable.
  • Choose the fabric type first, then the GSM, and confirm both on a swatch before sampling.
  • Specify fibre, GSM, brushed or unbrushed, pre-shrunk or not, and the finish in the tech pack.

What is fleece?

In a hoodie context, fleece usually means a knitted fabric with a smooth face and an inner side that has been brushed to raise a soft nap. The brushing traps air, which is why fleece feels warm. It is sometimes called brushed fleece or fleece-back jersey.

What is French terry?

French terry is also a knit with a smooth face, but the inside is left as small loops of yarn instead of being brushed. Some markets call it loopback. It is lighter than fleece at a similar weight, feels cooler against the skin and is popular for spring and autumn sweatshirts and activewear.

Fleece vs French terry at a glance

Fleece compared with French terry
Inner surfaceFleece: brushed, soft nap. French terry: unbrushed loops.
WarmthFleece is warmer. French terry is cooler and more breathable.
Typical useFleece: winter hoodies and sweatshirts. French terry: lighter hoodies, spring and autumn layers, activewear.
What to watchFleece: pilling and lint on the brushed side. French terry: loop snagging and shrinkage.

How to choose

Start with the season and the market. A winter range for a cold climate usually points to brushed fleece. A transitional or year-round range often suits French terry. Then think about positioning: heavier fabric reads as premium and holds a boxy shape, while lighter fabric drapes more and costs less to ship.

Weight is a separate decision. Two fabrics at the same GSM can feel very different because yarn, knit structure and finishing change the hand-feel. Our fleece fabric GSM guide explains how to brief the weight, and the hoodie and sweatshirt sourcing page lists the weight bands we usually work to.

What to put in the tech pack

  • Fabric type: brushed fleece or French terry.
  • Fibre composition, for example 100% cotton or a cotton-polyester blend.
  • GSM and an acceptable tolerance.
  • Whether the inside is brushed, and any anti-pilling or peach finish.
  • Whether the fabric is pre-shrunk, and the shrinkage tolerance you accept after washing.
  • Shade references for the body, rib and any contrast hood lining.

Print and embroidery

Both fabrics can be printed and embroidered, but they behave differently. A thick brushed fleece can need backing for embroidery, and a loopy inner face can affect how a print or patch sits. Test the decoration on the intended fabric and garment construction before bulk, and review it again after washing.

Sampling and quality checks

Compare the swatch for hand-feel against an approved reference, then check the sample for the points that fail first: shrinkage and twisting after washing, pilling, shade match between the body and rib, hood shape and trims. Agree the sampling route and the quality checkpoints before production starts.

Frequently asked questions

Is French terry a type of fleece?

Not strictly. Both are knitted fabrics used for hoodies and sweatshirts, but fleece has a brushed inner face and French terry has unbrushed loops. Some suppliers use the words loosely, so confirm which one is meant on a swatch.

Which is better for embroidery?

Either can work. The right choice depends on weight, stitch density and backing, so test the embroidery on the actual fabric and check it after a wash.
