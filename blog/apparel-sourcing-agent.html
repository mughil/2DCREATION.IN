What an apparel sourcing agent does, what it costs, and when to skip one

A buyer guide to using a sourcing agent in Tirupur: the five stages of the job, how agents are paid, and the questions to ask before you commit.

Written by the 2D Creation sourcing team in Tirupur, India, who coordinate product development, sampling, production follow-up and quality control for international apparel buyers.

Most apparel buyers do not fail because the factory was bad. They fail because nobody owned the gap between the tech pack, the sample, the bulk production and the packed carton. An apparel sourcing agent exists to own that gap. This guide explains exactly what an agent does, what it costs, when you should skip the agent and go direct, and the questions that separate a reliable partner from a middleman who only forwards emails.

Key takeaways

  • An agent is worth paying for when you lack a local team, source several categories, or order in volumes too small for a factory to prioritise.
  • Going direct to a factory is cheaper per unit only if you can absorb the time, travel and quality risk yourself.
  • Ask how the agent is paid. Commission, service fee and hidden mark-up each change who the agent is working for.
  • Agree the sample approval steps, the inspection standard and the escalation route in writing before the first order.

What an apparel sourcing agent actually does

A good agent works across five stages, and you should be able to ask which of them you are paying for.

  1. Brief review. The agent reads your tech pack or reference garment and flags what is missing: measurement points, fabric GSM, trim specifications, print placement, packing instructions.
  2. Factory matching. A T-shirt programme and a fleece hoodie programme need different machines and different skills. The agent shortlists factories that already make your product type, rather than factories that merely say yes.
  3. Development and sampling. The agent coordinates fabric and trim sourcing, pattern making and sample rounds, and keeps one approved reference sample so that bulk is judged against something physical.
  4. Production follow-up. The agent tracks cutting, sewing and finishing against the agreed timeline, and tells you early when a delay is coming.
  5. Quality control and shipment. Inline and final inspections, packing checks and shipping documents are coordinated before the goods leave the factory.

Agent, factory or trading company: which one are you talking to?

Buyers often use these three words as if they meant the same thing. They do not, and the difference decides who is accountable when something goes wrong.

When going direct to a factory is the better choice

If you place large, stable orders in a single product category, have your own technical and quality staff, and can visit Tirupur once or twice a year, working directly with a factory can lower your cost and shorten communication. An agent adds a layer you may not need.

When an agent earns its fee

  • You are a newer or growing brand and your quantity per style is below what a large factory wants to prioritise.
  • You buy across categories such as T-shirts, hoodies and kidswear, which are best made in different units.
  • You have no one on the ground to check sampling, cutting and packing.
  • You have been burnt before by sample-bulk mismatch or missed ship dates.

What it costs and how to read a quotation

Agents are usually paid by commission on order value or by a fixed service fee. Neither is wrong. What matters is that you know which one applies and whether the factory price in the quotation already includes it. Ask for a breakdown of fabric, trims, cut-make-trim, finishing, packing and agent fee. If an agent will not break the price down, treat that as the answer.

We charge a 10% sourcing and production coordination commission. It covers supplier sourcing, quotation, sampling coordination, production follow-up, quality coordination, documentation and shipment coordination.

Questions to ask any sourcing partner

  1. How do you choose a factory for a product like mine, and can I see two factories that already make it?
  2. How many sample rounds are included, and what happens if the sample misses the specification?
  3. Who inspects the goods, at which stages, and against which standard (for example AQL 2.5 for major defects)?
  4. How do you verify certificates such as GOTS or OEKO-TEX, and can you share the scope and validity date?
  5. What happens when a delay or defect appears, and how fast will I hear about it?
  6. Are payment terms tied to approvals, such as a deposit before bulk and the balance after inspection?

Transparent answers matter more than promises. If you only receive confident adjectives, keep looking.

Red flags in a sourcing agent

  • No factory names or visits, ever.
  • One price for every product, regardless of fabric or construction.
  • No written sample approval step.
  • A promise of any minimum order quantity before they have seen your product.

Frequently asked questions

Is 2D Creation a factory or a sourcing agent?

2D Creation is a sourcing agent based in Tirupur, Tamil Nadu. We do not own the factories. We match each product to a suitable manufacturer, then coordinate sampling, production follow-up and quality checks on your behalf.

How does an apparel sourcing agent charge?

Agents usually charge a commission on the order value or a fixed service fee, and the factory price is quoted separately. Ask for the fee structure in writing before you share a brief, and ask whether the factory price you see includes the agent's margin.

What is the minimum order quantity for a first order?

It depends on the fabric, the construction and the factory. Our typical MOQ is 300 pieces per style and colour. Send your brief and we confirm the MOQ for your product before any sampling starts.

Can I visit the factory before I order?

Yes. Buyers who want to see the production line can arrange a visit in Tirupur, and we can also share a factory profile and recent inspection reports for buyers who cannot travel.

What should I send to get a quotation?

Product type, a reference image or tech pack, target fabric and weight, quantity per style and colour, destination market and the date you need delivery. The more specific the brief, the fewer revision rounds you pay for.
